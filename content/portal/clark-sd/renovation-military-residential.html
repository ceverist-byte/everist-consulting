Section 01
Complex Overhead Coordination

What kills these projects in the ceiling — on any project type

All project types
01
Plans are clean in plan view. They break down in section.
Every conflict worth finding requires a vertical cut to see it. The floor plan passes a coordination review. The vertical stack does not. Commit to section cuts as the primary coordination artifact — not plan overlays. If you haven’t run a section cut at every beam-crossing condition, you haven’t coordinated.
02
Duct sizing is load-driven, not geometry-driven.
Hydraulically correct duct sizes are often physically uninstallable at plan ceiling heights once structural beam crossings are accounted for. Verify available plenum depth at every beam-crossing condition before fabrication submittals. The discrepancy between designed duct size and available depth can be substantial — sometimes requiring section-by-section renegotiation or beam penetrations to resolve. The M-series plan is internally consistent. It may not have been checked against the structure.
03
Ceiling assembly thickness and available plenum depth are two separate conversations.
The specified ceiling assembly and the space between structure and finish ceiling are rarely connected in the permit set. Verify the specified assembly against confirmed plenum depth independently — do not derive available space from the RCP alone. If the RCP and the structural drawings haven’t been overlaid in section, assume they haven’t been cross-checked.
04
Renovation projects
Existing overhead conditions are under-documented.
On any renovation with significant existing overhead infrastructure, build your model with existing conditions as unknowns — not as shown. The drawings often treat the building as empty before the renovation. It isn’t. Budget time for field verification before locking your model. Existing sanitary, waste, and storm drain routing frequently falls outside the documented envelope.
05
New structural elements sometimes reach the field without confirmed BOB elevations.
If the framing plan specifies new steel, get a field-verified or SEOR-confirmed bottom-of-beam elevation before modeling dependent systems. Scaling from the drawings or assuming typical depth from the section schedule is risky — the drawings themselves sometimes carry the instruction to verify. Don’t pass that uncertainty downstream to MEP.
Section 02
BEQ & Military Residential

What this project type adds

Military residential
01
Find the person with the overalls and a flashlight before you close the ceiling.
Facility maintenance personnel will live with what you build long after the construction team leaves. On military residential — BEQs especially — the overhead cavity must be navigable, marked, and documented for the people who will service it for the next 20 years. Get a facilities representative in the coordination conversation early. Their constraints are not in the spec.
02
Repeating floor plans multiply every unresolved conflict.
BEQs have recurring bays. A coordination problem in Bay 1 is the same problem in Bay 6 and Bay 12. Getting the prototype bay right before it repeats is not optional — it is the schedule. A sectional study on one typical bay, verified before fabrication, returns its cost many times over in avoided RFIs across the building.
03
Access panel locations, above-ceiling lighting, and grid markers belong in the model.
Not as afterthoughts. The maintenance experience of the building is a coordination deliverable. Above-ceiling lighting, column-grid markers, and adequate crawl clearances should be modeled and verified in section before the ceiling is closed. If the maintenance crew can’t locate where they are in a dark ceiling plenum, the coordination failed — regardless of what the clash report shows.
04
Confined ceiling access requires a protocol, not just a ladder.
On projects with occupied overhead — mechanical chases, equipment access zones, labyrinthine plenum routing — document the access sequence. Who enters. How they locate where they are. What clearance they need to bring tools. A dimensioned section cut at the tightest access condition is the minimum deliverable. A 6’4” maintenance tech trying to drag tools through a 36” plenum is a constructability failure that shows up on day one of occupancy, not during punch list.
